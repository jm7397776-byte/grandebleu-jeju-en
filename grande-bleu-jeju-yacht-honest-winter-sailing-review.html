Here's what to expect, what it costs, and why it works surprisingly well as a winter corporate outing.

Why a Catamaran Actually Matters in Winter

Winter in Jeju (December to February) is milder than most of Korea — daytime highs often sit around 8–12°C, and the southern coast around Seogwipo is the warmest patch on the island. It's the sort of "warm trip" that Hong Kong travellers used to Bali or Phuket sometimes underestimate until they're actually on deck with a beer in hand.

A monohull sailboat leans and pitches. A catamaran sits flat on two hulls, which means less rocking, more usable deck space, and — critically for a corporate group in their 50s — no one turning green ten minutes in. Grande Bleu is the only vessel of its kind certified in Korea, which is a small but real trust signal when you're putting a team on the water in winter.

Compared to a Greek islands day cruise or a Maldives sunset dhoni, the price-to-experience ratio here is genuinely competitive. You're paying roughly HK$400 for an hour that includes drinks most bars would charge that for on their own.

The 60-Minute Coastal Route

The route hugs the Seogwipo coastline, which is part of a UNESCO Global Geopark. In one hour you pass through:

  • Elephant Rock (Kkokkiribawi) — a cliff formation that genuinely looks like an elephant, not the usual "if you squint" tourist rock
  • Jingotnae Sea Canyon — vertical cliffs that drop straight into the water
  • Wolpyeong Basalt Columns — hexagonal volcanic sea cliffs, the same geology that got Jeju its UNESCO listing
  • Wolpyeong Sea Cave and the Large Sea Cave (Keunbada Donggul) — the biggest on this coast
  • Pangpaengideok — a wide flat reef plateau that photographs well in golden hour

The sunset itself lands as a 360-degree panorama — mountain behind, open sea ahead, cliffs on both sides. It happens once a day. That's the pitch, and it holds up.

Fishing Off the Deck

Every tour includes a real fishing session — rods and bait provided, no extra charge. You're going for genuine Jeju species: rockfish, scorpionfish, filefish, sometimes puffer. It's beginner-friendly, catch-and-release is available, and it's the sort of thing that breaks the ice in a corporate group faster than any conference-room activity.

Colleagues who wouldn't normally speak to each other end up comparing bait technique within twenty minutes. That's the slow, low-pressure side of Korean culture that doesn't make it into K-drama montages but is arguably the more memorable version.
