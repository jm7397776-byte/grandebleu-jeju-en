Why Jeju, Why a Yacht?

Most first-time visitors do Jeju by rental car — Hallasan hike, a couple of waterfalls, some cafés on the coast, done. It's fine, but you end up looking at the island the whole time, not from it.

The south coast around Seogwipo is where Jeju's volcanic weirdness actually shows up: hexagonal basalt columns, sea caves, cliffs shaped like elephants. From land you catch maybe 30% of it. From the water, you catch the rest.

Compared with a Bali sunset sail (party boat, loud) or a Greek islands catamaran (six hours, sunburn guaranteed), Jeju's version is short, calm, and — if you're travelling solo — genuinely easy to join without feeling like the odd one out.

What You Actually See in 60 Minutes

The route hugs the Seogwipo coastline west of Daepo Port. In one hour you pass:

  • Elephant Rock (Kkokkiribawi) — a cliff face that really does look like an elephant drinking
  • Jingotnae Sea Canyon — vertical walls plunging straight into the sea
  • Wolpyeong Basalt Columns — hexagonal volcanic cliffs, part of Jeju's UNESCO Global Geopark geology
  • Wolpyeong Sea Cave — a mysterious hollow the waves crash into
  • Pangpaengideok — a wide, flat reef plateau you can only appreciate from sea level
  • Large Sea Cave (Keunbada Donggul) — the biggest cave on this stretch of coast

You cannot walk to any of these. There is no viewpoint on land that shows you the full Wolpyeong column wall — it's only visible from the water.

The Catamaran Bit (Why It Matters)

Grande Bleu runs twin-hull catamarans, not single-hull yachts. In practice this means far less rocking — it's the only operator in Jeju certified to sail without life vests required, which tells you something about the stability rating.

If you get seasick easily, this is the one to pick. If you don't, you'll still appreciate being able to stand on deck with a beer and take photos without bracing yourself.

Licensed captain and engineer are onboard every sailing. The company partners with Shilla Hotel and Parnas Jeju, and has been running since 2011 with over 100,000 guests through the door.

Free Food, Free Drinks — Really

This is the part I was sceptical about. "Unlimited free drinks" on a tour boat usually means one warm can of beer and a biscuit.

Not here. Everything below is genuinely free and refillable for the whole hour:

  • Draft beer (Korean fresh brew, poured onboard)

  • Jeju tangerine juice (locally squeezed)
  • Bottled water
  • Jeju local cookies and traditional snacks

Bring nothing. Seriously. There's no gift shop pressure, no add-on menu.

Fishing, If You Feel Like It

Rods, bait and instruction are included. You might pull up rockfish (우럭), scorpionfish (쏨뱅이), filefish (쥐치) or puffer (복어) — all local Jeju species. Beginner-friendly, catch-and-release is fine, and the crew handle the fiddly bits.

If you'd rather just sit with a glass of wine, that's also completely fine. Nobody makes you fish.

Solo Travel in Spring: Why This Works

Jeju in March–May is the sweet spot. Cherry blossoms in early April, canola fields blazing yellow, temperatures 15–20°C — cool enough to enjoy sea wind, warm enough not to shiver on deck.

For solo travellers in your 20s, a Jeju sunset cruise fills the gap where dinner-for-one gets lonely. You're on a small vessel with 40-odd other people, mostly couples and friend groups, and the crew are used to solo guests. Nobody notices, nobody hassles you.

The golden hour lighting on the basalt columns happens once a day. It's the slow, quiet side of K-culture — no crowds, no queues, no K-pop soundtrack. Just the coast, the wind, and something cold in your hand.
